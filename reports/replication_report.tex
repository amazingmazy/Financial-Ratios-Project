\title{Predicting Returns with Financial Ratios:\\
       A Replication and Extension of Lewellen (2004)}
\author{Anthony Mazy \and Stefano Ramponi}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We replicate Lewellen (2004), which argues that the standard Stambaugh (1999)
small-sample bias correction understates the predictive power of financial
ratios, and that imposing the economically motivated bound $\rho \le 1$ yields
substantially stronger evidence of return predictability. We reproduce the
paper's dividend-yield results on CRSP data, extend the sample past its 2000
cutoff, and report where our numbers diverge and why.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Lewellen (2004) revisits a long-standing question in empirical finance:
whether financial ratios like the dividend yield (\(DY\)), book-to-market
(\(B/M\)), and earnings-price ratio (\(E/P\)) can predict aggregate stock
returns. Predictive regressions of this kind are known to suffer from a
small-sample bias, first characterized by Stambaugh (1986, 1999), that
arises because the predictor's own innovations are strongly correlated with
the return's innovations. The standard correction for this bias --
Stambaugh's marginal, or unconditional, small-sample distribution of the
slope estimate -- had become the accepted way to test for predictability,
and typically found weak evidence at best.

The paper's contribution is to show that this standard correction discards
useful information. Because \(DY\), \(B/M\), and \(E/P\) are all highly
persistent, stationarity itself puts an upper bound on their true
autocorrelation \(\rho\): it must be less than one. Conditioning on this
bound -- rather than integrating over all possible values of \(\rho\), as
the unconditional approach does -- produces a bias-adjusted estimator with a
closed-form Student-\(t\) distribution and a substantially smaller standard
error. Under the conservative assumption \(\rho \approx 1\), Lewellen finds
that dividend yield predicts NYSE returns strongly and consistently across
1946--2000 and its subperiods, in cases where the unconditional test finds
essentially nothing.

This project replicates the paper's central results -- the AR(1) regression
for \(\log(DY)\) and the three competing predictive-regression estimators
(OLS, Stambaugh, and the \(\rho \approx 1\) conditional test) -- on real
CRSP and Compustat data, extends the sample through the present, and
reports where our numbers agree with the paper, where they diverge, and
why. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data sources and construction.
Section~\ref{sec:replication} compares our replicated tables against the
paper's published values. Section~\ref{sec:extension} extends the sample to
2025 and discusses how the paper's own stated conditions for the
conditional test's power play out in the more recent data.
Section~\ref{sec:exhibits} presents our own summary statistics and figures.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} discusses successes and open issues.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

Returns, the risk-free rate, and index levels come from CRSP; book equity
and operating income come from Compustat, linked to CRSP via the CRSP/Compustat
Merged (CCM) linktable. We restrict the CRSP universe to NYSE-listed
securities throughout, using an exchange-code-filtered index rather than
CRSP's off-the-shelf composite index, since the latter is not NYSE-only
after NASDAQ's addition to CRSP's composite series in December 1972.

\paragraph{Dividend yield.} Following the paper (Section 3), we construct
the monthly dividend flow as \(\text{Div}_t \approx (\text{vwretd}_t -
\text{vwretx}_t) \times \text{TotVal}_{t-1}\), roll it to a trailing
12-month sum, and divide by the \emph{current} month's aggregate NYSE
market value to obtain \(DY_t\). All predictive regressions use
\(\log(DY)\) rather than the raw ratio, matching the paper's own choice:
raw \(DY\) is mechanically positively skewed and its volatility scales with
its level, while the log series is close to normal and better behaved for
the AR(1) specification the estimators assume.

\paragraph{Book-to-market and earnings-price ratios.} \(B/M\) and \(E/P\)
are constructed by aggregating Compustat book equity and operating income
before depreciation across all NYSE-listed, Compustat-covered firms with at
least three years of accounting history, grouped by fiscal year rather than
by exact fiscal year-end date (grouping by date scatters a single fiscal
year's cross-section of firms across dozens of near-single-firm groups,
since firms' fiscal year-ends differ). Accounting data is lagged at least
four months before being matched to a calendar month, so that no regression
ever uses information before it would have been publicly available.

Book equity itself admits more than one convention. We follow the
Fama-French definition, \(\text{CEQ} + \text{TXDITC} - \text{preferred
stock}\), as a starting point, but found that including deferred taxes
(\(\text{TXDITC}\)) inflates our aggregate \(B/M\) roughly 10\% above the
paper's published mean; dropping \(\text{TXDITC}\) closes nearly all of the
gap (Section~\ref{sec:replication}). Since Lewellen's paper specifies only
``the ratio of book equity to market equity'' without giving a formula, and
since the deferred-tax treatment is not settled even within the Fama-French
literature (it changed after FASB 109), we treat this as a documented,
deliberate choice rather than a bug, and default to excluding deferred
taxes as the variant that reproduces the paper.

\paragraph{Two CRSP schemas.} CRSP's legacy tables (SIZ) are frozen at
2024-12-31; the current Flat File 2.0 schema (CIZ), which replaced it in
January 2025, is the only one that reaches the present, but compounds
returns with dividends reinvested on the ex-date rather than SIZ's
month-to-month holding-period convention. We build both panels rather than
splicing them into a single series: the replication is run on SIZ, which
reproduces the paper's own return-compounding convention most closely, and
the extension to the present is run on CIZ, which is the only schema
capable of reaching it.

\paragraph{QA gate.} Before any predictive regression is run, the
constructed panel is checked against the paper's own Table 1 (mean,
standard deviation, skewness, and lag-1/12/24 autocorrelations for every
series). This QA step, not the predictive regressions themselves, is what
surfaced the data-construction issues discussed below.

\section{Replication}
\label{sec:replication}

We reproduce Tables 1 through 6 using the shared estimator library
(\texttt{src/estimators.py}): OLS, Stambaugh's (1999) Monte Carlo
bias-adjusted estimator, and Lewellen's closed-form \(\rho \approx 1\)
conditional test, together with the modified-Bonferroni joint test.

\paragraph{Dividend yield (Tables 1--3) replicates cleanly.} On the full
1946--2000 sample, our AR(1) regression for \(\log(DY)\) and the three
predictive-regression estimators for VWNY match the paper closely:

The OLS standard error matching to three decimal places is the strongest
single indicator that the index and dividend-flow construction is correct,
since it depends on both the variance of \(\log(DY)\) and the sample being
the same 660 months as the paper's. We formalize this comparison as an
automated test suite (\texttt{tests/test\_replication\_vs\_paper.py}, 320
parametrized assertions against Lewellen's published Tables 1, 2, 3, 5, and
6, at tolerances fixed \emph{before} results were seen and set according to
how each statistic propagates into the final estimates -- autocorrelation
is gated tightly, since a 0.01 error in \(\hat\rho\) can move the
bias-adjusted slope by roughly 0.9 and flip the sign of the paper's
headline result).

\paragraph{Book-to-market and earnings-price ratios (Tables 5--6) required
resolving a real definitional ambiguity.} Our initial pull of \(B/M\)
averaged 58.69 over 1963--2000 against the paper's published 53.13, a
10.5\% gap; \(E/P\) diverged about half as much. Tracing the asymmetry --
an error in the firm-level accounting pull should hit both ratios roughly
equally, while an error specific to the book-equity \emph{formula} should
hit only \(B/M\) -- pointed to deferred taxes (\(\text{TXDITC}\)), which
make up 12.1\% of our aggregate book equity under the standard
Fama-French definition. Excluding them brings the mean to 52.08, within
2\% of the paper, though a residual gap of roughly 5\% remains in both
ratios, attributable to the paper's unspecified ``three years of accounting
history'' screen and 20+ years of subsequent Compustat data restatement.
Per the assigned scope, \(B/M\) (Table 5) is the designated deliverable;
\(E/P\) (Table 6) is carried as a robustness check.

\section{Extension}
\label{sec:extension}

Extending the sample requires the CIZ schema, since CRSP's legacy tables do
not reach past 2024. On the full extended sample, \textbf{the paper's
conclusion survives}: for 1946--2025 (VWNY), OLS gives \(\hat b = 0.862\)
(\(p = 0.005\)), Stambaugh's unconditional correction gives \(\hat b =
0.411\) (\(p = 0.186\)), and the \(\rho \approx 1\) conditional test gives
\(\hat b = 0.413\) (\(t = 3.50\), \(p = 0.000\)) -- the same qualitative
pattern the paper is built around, twenty-five years past its original
cutoff: the unconditional test fails to reject while the conditional test
rejects decisively.

Splitting the extension at 2001 is more interesting, and is not a
counterexample to the paper. Dividend yield's autocorrelation falls to
0.9515 in the 2001--2025 subsample, below the paper's own stated threshold
(Section 2.4) of roughly 0.99 for a 25-year monthly sample -- and the
conditional test's power collapses accordingly: \(\hat b = -0.590\) (\(t =
-1.28\), \(p = 0.899\)), even as OLS reports \(\hat b = 3.862\) (\(p =
0.006\)). This is exactly the relationship Lewellen's own Table A.1 power
simulation predicts: the conditional test's power falls toward zero as
\(\rho\) drops away from one, and the bias-adjusted estimate is biased
downward once \(\rho\) is genuinely below the assumed 1. Rather than
contradicting the paper, the post-2000 subsample demonstrates the
limitation the paper itself states, and the autocorrelation is the number
to read first when interpreting it -- a more than fourfold increase in the
OLS slope over the same window (0.862 to 3.862) with a stable $p$-value is
the small-sample bias the paper is about, made visible by a shorter,
less-persistent sample rather than by any change in the underlying
economics.

As a robustness check, we also compare the SIZ and CIZ schemas over an
identical 1946--2024 window (truncating CIZ to match SIZ's endpoint, so the
comparison is not confounded by sample length). The two schemas' results
differ modestly -- e.g.\ \(\text{corr}(e,m)\) of \(-0.955\) (SIZ) vs.\
\(-0.937\) (CIZ) -- consistent with CIZ's ex-date dividend-reinvestment
convention differing from SIZ's month-end convention, but the qualitative
conclusion is unchanged under either schema.

\section{Our Own Exhibits}
\label{sec:exhibits}

Lewellen's entire argument depends on \(\log(DY)\) staying close enough to a
unit root that the \(\rho \approx 1\) conditional test has power -- he
states the requirement explicitly (Section 2.4): roughly 0.98 monthly
autocorrelation for a 25-year sample, 0.99 for 50 years. His own sample
ends in 2000, so the paper never asks whether that condition keeps holding.
We do, using the extended CIZ panel built for
Section~\ref{sec:extension}.

Table~\ref{tab:own-decades} reports mean, standard deviation, and lag-1
autocorrelation (\(\hat\rho\)) of \(\log(DY)\), \(\log(B/M)\), and
\(\log(E/P)\) by decade. \(\log(DY)\)'s persistence is not flat across the
sample: persistence varies through the 2000s and before but declines by the 2010s, roughly
matching the crossing shown in Figure~\ref{fig:own-rho}.

The point of both exhibits is the same: the paper's conditional test is
powerful precisely because \(DY\) has historically been extremely
persistent, and that is an empirical fact about the twentieth-century
sample the paper studies, not a property guaranteed to hold indefinitely.
Whether it continues to hold is exactly the question a reader deciding
whether to trust the conditional test on \emph{current} data needs
answered, and it is not one Lewellen's own tables can address.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The dividend-yield replication (Tables 1--3) is essentially exact, with the
OLS standard error matching the paper to three decimal places and every
estimator -- OLS, Stambaugh, and the \(\rho \approx 1\) conditional test --
reproducing both the point estimates and the paper's central finding that
the conditional test rejects the null far more decisively than the
unconditional one. The main challenge was in the book-to-market and
earnings-price construction, where an unstated definitional choice --
whether to include deferred taxes in book equity -- accounted for most of
an initial 10\% discrepancy; resolving it required treating the paper's own
ambiguity as a flag rather than assuming a single correct formula. The
extension to 2025 both confirms the paper's headline result over the full
sample and illustrates its own stated limitation once dividend yield's
autocorrelation falls below the paper's power threshold post-2000 -- a
result the paper's Table A.1 predicts rather than one that undermines it.
Our own exhibits (Section~\ref{sec:exhibits}) extend that point one step
further, showing directly where and when that decline in persistence
occurs, which is the piece of the picture the paper's own 1946--2000 sample
cannot show.
